Some people are remarkably certain they know who other people are. A few conversations, some familiar behavior, or fragments of someone's past are enough for them to form an opinion. Once that picture takes shape, they rarely stop to consider how much they still don't know.

When someone thinks in ways they can't follow, uses unfamiliar concepts, or expresses ideas outside their usual frame of reference, something curious happens. Instead of questioning their own understanding, they question the person in front of them.

Suddenly, that person is strange, difficult, or even intellectually inferior. But based on what, exactly?

Struggling to understand an idea doesn't mean the idea makes no sense. It doesn't mean the person expressing it is right, either. At that point, all we really know is that there's a gap between what one person is trying to communicate and what another person can make of it.

**The problem starts when we turn that gap into a judgment about someone else's intelligence without first examining the limits of our own.**

## The Illusion of Knowing Someone

Knowing someone for years can give us a powerful sense of familiarity. We know their habits, their interests, how they react in certain situations, and the opinions they've expressed. That information is real. It's also only part of who they are.

Yet we often treat that part as if it were the whole person.

In 2001, Emily Pronin and her colleagues investigated what they called the *illusion of asymmetric insight*. Across six studies, people tended to believe they knew their peers better than their peers knew them.

The researchers identified a revealing difference in perspective. We see our private thoughts and feelings as essential to understanding ourselves, but we often give other people's observable behavior more weight than we give our own when deciding what it says about their character. [Pronin and colleagues, 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11642351/).

That asymmetry can make us far too confident in what we think we know about someone else.

We can know a person's habits without understanding their motivations. We can recognize familiar patterns of behavior while knowing very little about their intellectual interests. We can even be close to someone and remain unaware of entire dimensions of how they think.

Familiarity does not guarantee depth of understanding.

Psychologist David Funder proposed a model in 1995 for explaining when judgments about personality are accurate. Relevant clues have to exist, be available to the observer, be noticed, and then be interpreted correctly. [Funder, 1995](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.652).

Our impressions, then, are not automatically false. We really can know things about other people. But that knowledge depends on what we've actually observed and whether we've understood it accurately.

Seeing a behavior a hundred times doesn't mean we know exactly what it reveals.

Here's the unsettling part: **an incomplete picture can feel complete simply because it makes sense to us.**

We don't have to know much about someone to become convinced we know exactly who they are.

## Your Intelligence Is Not a Universal Standard

There is an uncomfortable fact that often gets lost in conversations about understanding people: we do not all have identical intellectual abilities.

Differential psychology has spent decades studying individual differences in abstract reasoning, verbal comprehension, working memory, acquired knowledge, and other cognitive abilities.

[The Cattell, Horn, and Carroll framework](https://www.guilford.com/books/Contemporary-Intellectual-Assessment/Flanagan-McDonough/9781462552030/contents) distinguishes several of these abilities, including fluid reasoning, which helps us solve unfamiliar problems, and crystallized knowledge, which reflects what we've learned through education and experience.

Those differences matter. People do not all have the same resources for processing information, spotting complex relationships, or following an abstract argument.

It would be naive to pretend every failure to understand something is simply a difference in interests or life experience.

But a distinction matters just as much.

**Not knowing a concept is not the same as being unable to understand it. And being capable of understanding something does not mean you actually understood it.**

Someone may not know a technical term but grasp the idea as soon as it's explained in familiar language. Someone else may have an impressive vocabulary and still reason badly about what those words mean.

Complex language is not proof of deep thinking, either.

Psychologist Raymond Nickerson examined how we estimate what other people know. His 1999 review showed that we often use our own knowledge as a starting point, sometimes assuming other people know what we know. [Nickerson, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.737).

What seems obvious to us may not be obvious to anyone else.

The reverse can happen, too. We may encounter an argument we cannot follow because we lack the relevant background, conceptual tools, or cognitive resources at that moment.

Acknowledging that limit would be a reasonable intellectual response. Dismissing the argument before we've understood it is something else.

**Your inability to follow an idea is not a refutation of that idea.**

Failing to understand someone doesn't prove they're smarter than you. But it also gives you no basis for declaring them unintelligent. Before passing judgment on their ability, you have to confront a less flattering possibility: perhaps your own understanding hasn't gone far enough.

Your mind can be a tool for evaluating arguments. It is not, without further evidence, a universal measure of what other people are capable of thinking.

## The Danger of Thinking You Know Enough

In October 2024, Hunter Gehlbach, Carly Robinson, and Angus Fletcher published a study of the *illusion of information adequacy*.

In an experiment involving 1,261 participants, people considered a hypothetical decision about whether two schools should merge. Some received all the relevant arguments. Others saw only about half of them.

Those who received partial information still believed they had adequate information, and they considered themselves just as competent to make a thoughtful decision as those who had seen everything. Yet their decisions were strongly shaped by whichever half they had seen. [Gehlbach and colleagues, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0310216).

The study did not measure intelligence or how accurately we judge another person's personality. Its relevance here is more specific: **we do not always notice the information we're missing before we form an opinion.**

Missing information doesn't necessarily feel like a gap. Sometimes we build a story that fits everything we've seen and mistake that internal consistency for proof that the story is true.

Imagine knowing someone almost entirely through casual social settings. You've seen them joke around, talk about everyday things, and act comfortably among friends. Over time, you settle on an image of who they are.

Then you discover interests you never knew they had, difficult questions they've spent years thinking about, or expertise in an area you barely understand.

That new information may not fit the person you thought you knew.

You can revise your impression and admit you had only seen one side of them. Or you can dismiss what doesn't fit as strange, exaggerated, or ridiculous.

In the second case, the problem may have little to do with the person you're observing. It may be your difficulty making room for evidence that your original picture left out.

**The problem isn't that we form incomplete pictures of people. We have no choice but to do that. The problem is acting as if those pictures are complete.**

## When Your Mind Becomes the Measure of Others

![Narcissus bending over still dark water and staring at his own reflected image](/images/caravaggio-narcissus.jpg)

*Narcissus, attributed to Caravaggio, around 1599. Here the reflection serves as a metaphor: sometimes we think we're looking at someone else when our own perspective is all we can see. [Image source and rights](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Michelangelo_Merisi_da_Caravaggio_-_Narcissus_-_WGA04109.jpg).*

Philosopher Gadamer developed an account of understanding that helps explain why our perspective matters so much.

For Gadamer, interpretation always takes place within a horizon shaped by history, language, knowledge, and experience. We do not encounter the world from a neutral position untouched by everything we've learned.

But our horizons are not fixed. Encountering something unfamiliar can challenge our assumptions and change what we're able to understand. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Gadamer](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/gadamer/).

Social psychology approaches a related problem through *naive realism*: the tendency to experience our own interpretation of events as if it were simply reality itself. [Gehlbach and colleagues, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0310216).

When someone thinks differently, we can ask what experiences, knowledge, or arguments brought them to that position. Or we can assume they must be wrong because they don't see things the way we do.

At that point, something shifts.

**We stop using our understanding to examine another person and start using that person to reassure ourselves that our understanding is correct.**

What we don't understand becomes a flaw we assign to them. Instead of examining who they are, we try to make them fit the categories we already have.

We may even convince ourselves that our difficulty understanding someone proves they lack intelligence.

But having an opinion and having a justified opinion are two different things. Confidence does not establish accuracy. Neither does a long history with someone.

**You may be intelligent enough to form an opinion about another person. That doesn't mean you understand them. And it certainly doesn't mean you're right.**

## The Arrogance of Not Knowing Your Own Limits

![A contemplative Aristotle resting his hand on a sculpted bust of Homer in a dark interior](/images/rembrandt-aristotle-homer.jpg)

*Aristotle with a Bust of Homer, Rembrandt, 1653. This imagined portrait of a philosopher offers a visual pause before the question of how accurately we judge our own thinking. Image courtesy of The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Open Access, CC0. [Museum record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/437394).*

So far, we've talked about cognitive ability, prior knowledge, and missing information. But there's another issue that may matter just as much: how well we evaluate our own reasoning.

Psychologists study this under *metacognition*.

It's one thing to answer a question. It's another to know how confident you should be in that answer, and whether your confidence matches your actual performance.

Two people can make the same mistake. One admits they're unsure. The other is certain they've gotten it right.

Research on intellectual humility adds another layer.

Across five studies involving 1,189 participants, Elizabeth Krumrei-Mancuso and colleagues examined how intellectual humility relates to knowledge and learning. It was associated with more general knowledge and with dispositions that favor learning, such as curiosity, reflective thinking, and open-mindedness. It was also associated with less claiming of knowledge one doesn't actually have. But it was unrelated to cognitive ability, and the results on metacognition were mixed: intellectual humility also went along with underestimating one's own cognitive ability. [Krumrei-Mancuso and colleagues, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439760.2019.1579359).

Later, Joseph Leman, Courtney Kurinec, and Wade Rowatt examined how intellectual humility relates to judging one's own performance. Across two studies, people who performed poorly tended to overestimate how well they had done, and that tendency was stronger among participants lower in intellectual humility. Intellectual humility itself was unrelated to how well people actually performed. [Leman and colleagues, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439760.2021.1975155).

These findings help separate two abilities that are easy to confuse.

One is the ability to solve problems, process information, and follow complex reasoning. The other is the ability to recognize how much you actually know and how well you're doing.

**You can struggle intellectually with an idea. You can also struggle to recognize that you haven't understood it.**

The second problem becomes especially consequential when you start judging other people.

Someone who recognizes what they don't know has a reason to ask questions, look for information, and reconsider a conclusion. Someone who believes they already understand enough may see no reason to do any of that.

That's where intellectual arrogance becomes more than simple ignorance. It is confidence that outruns what your knowledge and evidence can support.

And when that confidence enters our relationships, we may start attributing flaws to other people that we haven't actually established.

**Sometimes you're not discovering the limits of someone else's intellect. You're revealing your own without even noticing.**

## Understanding Takes More Than Feeling Certain

In 2018, Tal Eyal, Mary Steffel, and Nicholas Epley reported 25 experiments testing whether imagining another person's perspective improves the accuracy of our judgments about their thoughts, feelings, and preferences.

They found no consistent evidence that simply trying to see things from another person's point of view improved accuracy. If anything, accuracy tended to decrease, while confidence sometimes increased.

In a final experiment, directly obtaining another person's perspective through conversation did improve accuracy. [Eyal and colleagues, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000115).

That difference matters.

You can spend hours imagining what someone thinks, developing theories about their motives, and assembling an explanation of their personality. None of that guarantees you're right if the information you're working with is incomplete.

Sometimes you need something your own reasoning cannot supply: new information.

That means allowing for an uncomfortable possibility. The version of someone you've carried around in your head may be wrong.

Not because you were necessarily irrational from the start, but because you were drawing conclusions from a limited point of view.

Critical thinking doesn't mean questioning only what other people say. It means holding your own conclusions to the same standard.

**Judging someone is easy. Knowing whether you've understood enough to justify that judgment is much harder.**

## The Limit You Refuse to See

Our minds have to interpret the world. Forming impressions of people is an unavoidable part of social life.

Interpretation isn't the problem.

The problem begins when we stop distinguishing between a person and our interpretation of that person.

When we confuse familiarity with deep knowledge. When we mistake our vocabulary for the limits of what can be thought. When we treat our own intellectual experience as the standard everyone else should meet.

And especially when we turn our own failure to understand something into a verdict about someone else's abilities.

Not understanding an idea doesn't make it right. But it doesn't give you grounds to dismiss it as nonsense, either.

Not understanding a person doesn't make them extraordinary. But it doesn't make your limited impression of them an adequate definition of who they are.

Intellectual ability matters. Knowledge matters. The information available to us matters. So does recognizing the limits of all three.

We can be wrong about someone without knowing it. We can know them for years without ever encountering important parts of how they think. We can interpret their words through categories too narrow to capture what they're trying to say.

And we can make every one of those mistakes while feeling absolutely certain of ourselves.

So before calling someone incomprehensible, strange, or intellectually limited, consider a possibility that's a lot less comfortable.

**How many times have you judged someone because you couldn't understand them, without considering that the real limit might be your own intellectual ability?**
