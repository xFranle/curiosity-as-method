There is something peculiar about the way we look at the Moon. Its presence is familiar, yet what it means to us can change profoundly over the course of a life.

We might look up at it on a quiet evening, on the way home, or during one of those late nights spent working, thinking, or trying to find our bearings. Such moments may be years apart, yet the same familiar presence seems to accompany us as our story unfolds.

The Moon knows nothing of our circumstances. It does not know what we have lived through or how far we have walked. Perhaps it does not need to know for us to find in it something more than beauty.

In *Luna tucumana*, by Atahualpa Yupanqui and Pablo del Cerro, the Moon becomes more than a light in the night sky. It accompanies a traveler along the road, through solitude and through moments when even the next direction is uncertain. Its meaning lies not only in how it shines, but in the journey it seems to have witnessed.

There is something deeply human about seeing it this way.

Over time, places, songs, and landscapes acquire meanings that go far beyond their physical features. A street can bring back a period of our lives. A melody can return us to a moment we thought we had forgotten. And the Moon, seen from so many places under so many different circumstances, can become a familiar presence through which we recognize what we have been through.


Yet there is another side to that same Moon.

For just as it may accompany the paths we have already walked, it may also lead us to imagine the places on it where we have not yet set foot.

Perhaps those two ways of looking at the Moon are closer than they first appear.

## The nights that stay with us

Not every night leaves the same kind of memory.

Some unfold in conversations we wish would last a little longer. Others become associated with the beginning of a project, an important decision, or the hours we spend awake trying to understand something that has not yet become clear.

Many pass almost unnoticed.

And yet, as time goes by, certain moments come to mean something we could not have recognized while living them.

A late night of work may become a memory of where something important began. A familiar route may recall a chapter of our lives that has ended. Even a night spent in uncertainty may look different once we know what happened afterward.

The Moon can belong to those memories. Not because it preserves them for us, but because its recurring presence offers a thread between otherwise distant moments.

In [*The Moon*](https://www.gaceta.unam.mx/la-luna-un-poema-de-borges/), a poem included in *El hacedor* (1960), Jorge Luis Borges explores the many ways people have tried to represent the Moon through language, myth, and literature. His poem also approaches a difficulty: however many images we make of the Moon, something about it seems to remain beyond the words we use.

Perhaps that is part of our relationship with it, too.

We can describe its light, its shape, or the landscape around it. None of those descriptions necessarily captures what it means to someone looking at it through the history of their own life.

One Moon can accompany countless lives and take on a different meaning in each.

For one person it may recall someone they love. For another, a place they hope to return to. It may also bring back those nights when we worked toward something that still felt far away, when the outcome of our efforts lay beyond our reach.

Its meaning can change even for the same person.

Years later, we may look up again and remember who we were when we stood beneath that sky under entirely different circumstances. The Moon may be hanging over the same rooftops or the same streets, but we now carry experiences we did not have back then.

Sometimes, in encountering something that remains, we discover how much we ourselves have changed.

Yupanqui's Moon does not literally know the traveler's story. It is the traveler who recognizes in it a presence that has been part of the road. The meaning grows from the encounter between something that endures and everything we have lived while looking at it.

But our story is not made only of what we remember.

It is also made of all that we have yet to live.

## The other side of the Moon

From Earth, we see essentially the same face of the Moon. Because its rotation is synchronized with its orbit around our planet, much of its surface remains hidden from our direct view. Over time, a slight apparent wobble, or *libration*, lets us see a little more than half of its surface, but not the far side in full. [NASA explains this motion](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/).

We can spend an entire lifetime looking at the Moon without seeing all of it from where we stand.

There is something striking about knowing that so much exists beyond our familiar view.

We recognize the Moon's phases and the way it appears over the horizon. Still, familiarity with one side does not mean familiarity with the whole.

Perhaps something similar is true of our lives.

We know the paths we have taken, the experiences we have had, and the decisions that helped shape who we are. We know what we have done and something of the abilities we have developed along the way.

But what we have lived is not necessarily the full extent of what we may yet live.

There are places we have not visited, experiences we have not had, and possibilities we already recognize as our own but have not been able to make real.

It is not always a matter of discovering something we never knew existed.

Sometimes we have a clear sense of where we want to go. We have acquired knowledge, gained experience, and developed good reasons to trust our abilities. We may recognize a possibility long before we find ourselves in a position to pursue it.

And still, some paths remain unwalked.

The far side of the Moon offers an image of that distance. We know it exists. We can study it, picture its terrain, and learn about its surface without ever having traveled there ourselves.


But knowing a place exists is not the same as experiencing what it is like to be there.

Likewise, we may know what we are capable of without yet having the circumstances that would allow us to put those abilities into practice.

In that case, taking a new path is not necessarily a test of a confidence we never had. It may be the beginning of a life we have long known was possible.

## When we can finally begin

There are times when our abilities and our circumstances seem to move at different speeds.

We can keep learning, working, and building knowledge while certain parts of our lives remain on hold. This is not always a question of willpower, nor does it necessarily mean we do not know who we are or what we want.

Sometimes we know what we want to develop, but the conditions for doing so in a way that feels true to ourselves have not yet come together.

In [*Meditations on Quixote*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/57448) (1914), José Ortega y Gasset argues that we cannot understand a human life in isolation from its circumstances. Our decisions take place amid relationships, responsibilities, opportunities, and limitations. We do not choose from nowhere.

And even when our abilities are real, the room we have to exercise them can change over time.

Centuries earlier, Aristotle developed the distinction between [potentiality and actuality](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-metaphysics/)—between a capacity and its actual exercise. His distinction belongs to a much broader metaphysical account, but it helps illuminate a simple difference in everyday life: having an ability is not the same as having the opportunity to use it.

This does not mean that everything we hope for is bound to happen. It means only that our present life does not necessarily express all the possibilities for which we have developed the abilities.

Sometimes years pass between recognizing a possibility and finally beginning to act on it.

In the meantime, we keep living. We make decisions, meet people, learn from experience, and develop skills without knowing precisely where they will lead.

And the nights keep passing, too.

The nights we devote to something we want to build. The ones we share with others. The ones in which we try to understand where we are and where we want to go next.

Sometimes we look up and find the Moon.

The same Moon we saw when our projects were little more than ideas. The same one that accompanied the seasons when we had more questions than answers.

With time, some circumstances change. Certain decisions become possible, and parts of our lives that once had to remain in the background begin to find their place.

We may not have reached where we hoped to go. But something has shifted: what we spent so long considering a possibility is beginning to become part of our experience.

The path we once imagined is now taking shape beneath our feet.

## Going around the Moon

Antonio Machado wrote of the road being made in the very act of walking it. In [*Proverbios y cantares*](https://poemas.uned.es/poema/proverbios-y-cantares-antonio-machado/), the traveler does not discover a fully formed road laid out in advance.

That image helps us understand a distinction: we may know where we want to go without knowing everything the journey will ask of us.

Having a direction does not mean that the path is already written.

We can spend years preparing for something, understanding our abilities, studying the difficulties ahead, and building the conditions we need. Yet there is a part of every journey that can be known only by living it.

Perhaps that is why the thought of traveling around the Moon is so compelling.

For years we may have looked at its familiar face from different windows, cities, and moments of our lives. We may have gathered memories beneath it while time passed. And still, we can imagine moving beyond the horizon we have always seen.

Walking somewhere we have never walked before.

Not because the Moon we knew has lost its meaning, but because there are other ways of experiencing it.

Something similar can happen with our own paths. We may have known for a long time where we wanted to arrive—not as a baseless fantasy, but as a possibility grounded in our abilities, in what we have learned, and in what we have built.

Recognizing it from a distance, however, is not the same as traveling there.

There is an experience that begins only when we stop looking exclusively at the path and start walking it.

This need not mean risking everything or moving without direction. Nor must every decision become a demonstration of courage. Sometimes it means that our actions can finally express something we have recognized in ourselves for years.

And so we begin to travel around that part of our own Moon we had never known through experience.

Without abandoning its familiar face. Without forgetting the nights that brought us here.

The paths we have already walked remain with us—in what we know, in what we have lived, and in the ways we have learned to understand the world.

Perhaps what allows us to move forward now began taking shape much earlier, on those very nights when we did not yet know when we would be able to begin.

There may be a particular kind of happiness in that beginning. Not only because of the place we hope to reach, but because we are finally walking a path that was, for so long, only a possibility.

## Beneath the same Moon

It is strange to think that some nights we remember now were entirely ordinary when we lived them.

We could not know what they would come to mean, or how the decisions and experiences ahead would change our lives. We were simply living—working late, sharing a conversation, or walking home with thoughts of what we wanted to build.

And on some of those nights, the Moon was there.

Time passed. Some things changed; others remained. And certain possibilities that once seemed distant began to take shape.

Then, on an ordinary night, we may look up and find it again.

It is the same Moon. But now we know something we did not know then: what came after those nights, where our steps eventually led, and which of our hopes we managed to turn into experience.

There is something meaningful in that continuity.

The Moon can accompany us when we are still trying to find our direction and appear again when we begin to follow it. It can belong to our memories without belonging only to the past, because we keep encountering it as our lives continue.

On some nights it may remind us of what we have lived. On others, of what we are building. Sometimes both seem to meet in a single glance.

Perhaps this is one reason it is so difficult to say exactly what the Moon means to us. We are not looking at a distant object alone. We are also encountering something of our own history—who we have been, and the possibilities that have yet to become experience.

And that story is still unfolding.

Part of the Moon is familiar to us. It has been a symbolic companion to our nights, our efforts, and the moments we now hold in memory. But we can also imagine what lies beyond its horizon.

Not necessarily an impossible destination or an adventure without direction. Perhaps simply the paths we have long recognized as possible, even when we had not yet found the moment to take them.

And one night, after we have begun along one of those paths, we will look up again.

The Moon will still be there.

The same one we looked at when we did not yet know when we could begin.

Only now, another part of the journey will be behind us. And ahead of us, there will still be a horizon.

**Perhaps we do not need to find another Moon. Perhaps the time has come to explore the part of the same Moon that has always accompanied us, but where we have never yet walked.**
